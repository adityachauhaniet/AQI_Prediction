AQI Standards

Understanding Global Air Quality Guidelines

Air quality standards define how clean the air must be to protect human health. These standards are set by international and national agencies based on extensive scientific research linking air pollution exposure to serious health outcomes.

While air pollution is a global problem, AQI standards differ across countries due to variations in policy priorities, economic conditions, population density, and monitoring capabilities. This page explains how WHO, India, and the United States define and regulate air quality—and what these differences mean for public health.


What Are AQI Standards?

AQI standards specify the maximum allowable concentration of major air pollutants, including:

  • PM2.5 (fine particulate matter)
  • PM10 (coarse particulate matter)
  • Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂)
  • Ozone (O₃)
  • Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂)
  • Carbon Monoxide (CO)

The Air Quality Index (AQI) converts these pollutant concentrations into a simple scale that indicates how polluted the air is and how it may affect health.

Higher AQI values indicate worse air quality and greater health risks.


WHO Air Quality Guidelines

The World Health Organization (WHO) provides global air quality guidelines that are widely considered the health-based benchmark.

Key Highlights:

  • WHO guidelines are not legally binding
  • Designed to minimize health risks for all populations
  • Based on decades of global epidemiological studies

In 2021, WHO significantly tightened its guidelines, reflecting new evidence that air pollution causes harm even at very low levels.

WHO PM2.5 Guideline:

  • Annual average: 5 µg/m³
  • 24-hour average: 15 µg/m³

WHO states that no level of air pollution is completely safe, and lower concentrations always lead to better health outcomes.


India’s AQI Standards (CPCB – NAAQS)

India follows the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS), regulated by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB).

Key Highlights:

  • Legally enforceable
  • Designed for India’s environmental and socio-economic conditions
  • Uses a six-category AQI system

India PM2.5 Standard:

  • Annual average: 40 µg/m³
  • 24-hour average: 60 µg/m³

India’s standards are less strict than WHO guidelines, meaning air classified as “acceptable” under Indian AQI may still pose long-term health risks.


United States AQI Standards (EPA – NAAQS)

The United States regulates air quality through the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) under the Clean Air Act.

Key Highlights:

  • Strict enforcement and regular scientific review
  • Strong protection for sensitive groups
  • Widely adopted AQI communication system

US PM2.5 Standard:

  • Annual average: 9 µg/m³
  • 24-hour average: 35 µg/m³

The US AQI scale (0–500) is used globally and serves as the foundation for many international AQI platforms.

Why Do AQI Standards Differ?

Air quality standards are shaped by:

  • Scientific evidence available at the time of regulation
  • Monitoring and enforcement capacity
  • Economic and industrial considerations
  • Population exposure and vulnerability

WHO guidelines represent ideal health protection, while national standards often reflect practical implementation limits.


What Do These Differences Mean for Health?

Research consistently shows that:

  • Long-term exposure to PM2.5 below national limits can still cause harm
  • Health risks increase with duration of exposure, not just peak levels
  • Children, elderly people, and those with heart or lung disease are most affected

Aligning national standards closer to WHO guidelines can significantly reduce:

  • Premature deaths
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Respiratory illness
  • Economic healthcare burden
